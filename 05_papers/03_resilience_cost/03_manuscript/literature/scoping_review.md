# Scoping review by hypothesis — The cost of resilience

Status: 14 November 2026. Working document for the paper stage. The report (`../cost_of_resilience.qmd`) has not been changed; the rewording in section 5 is a proposal.

Companion files in this folder:

- `evidence_table.md` — every charted source, one table per scoping question (277 rows, 217 distinct sources).
- `references.bib` — one entry per source, built from Crossref records.
- `search_logs/` — the four search logs (queries, tools, exclusions, access problems, gaps).

## 1. How to read this document

- **Labels.** This review uses the hypothesis labels of the report as it stood when the search ran. The report was restructured later the same day: measurement validity is no longer a hypothesis, so the material under H3 and RQ3 here feeds the report's robustness section, and H4 here is H3 in the report.
- The form is a scoping review: it maps what has been studied on each hypothesis and what was found. It does not rate study quality and it does not claim to be exhaustive. The PRISMA-ScR checklist has not been filled in; that stays with the author.
- Of the 277 charted entries, 44 rest on the full text, 217 on the abstract only, and 16 on the bibliographic record only. **A finding read from an abstract must be checked in the full text before it is cited for a number or a method.** The evidence table states the read scope of every row.
- "Supports", "qualifies" and "contradicts" refer to the hypotheses as worded in the report on 14 November 2026.

## 2. Protocol

**Scoping questions.** One per hypothesis, plus one on antecedents.

| | Question |
|---|---|
| Q0 | Has the life-satisfaction gap of resilient students already been reported, in PISA or elsewhere? |
| H1 | What is known about the wellbeing of disadvantaged students who achieve highly? |
| H2 | How do socio-emotional skills and relationships relate to adolescents' life satisfaction, and is there evidence that they mask other gaps? |
| H3 | What is known about response styles and careless responding in PISA-type questionnaires and their relation to achievement? |
| H4 | Does the achievement–wellbeing association differ by family background, and what does the social-mobility literature say? |
| RQ1 | Do school composition, peer comparison, gender or aspirations condition wellbeing among high achievers? |
| RQ2 | Which specific skills have been tied to wellbeing costs or benefits? |
| RQ3 | How has reporting heterogeneity in life-satisfaction scales been handled, and do standards for a top answer differ by education or achievement? |

**Eligibility.** School-age children and adolescents; young adults and adults admitted for the social-mobility, skin-deep-resilience and reporting-heterogeneity literatures, where little else exists. Peer-reviewed articles, books, OECD and official reports; working papers, preprints and theses admitted and flagged. English. 2000–2026, with earlier foundational works.

**Sources searched.** Web search, Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar, ERIC, RePEc, publisher and repository pages. Scopus and Web of Science were not available.

**Method.** Keyword searches in several phrasings per question, selection of core papers, backward chaining from their reference lists. Each source entered the chart only after a page for it was opened in the session.

**Charting fields.** Reference with DOI; data and country; sample; design; outcome measure; finding with direction; bearing on the hypothesis; read scope; method notes for sources read in full.

## 4. Findings by question

### Q0. Antecedents

The direction of the raw gap is not new. Three things have already been published.

- **OECD (2019), PISA 2018 Volume II, chapter 3.** Resilient students are defined as bottom-quarter status and top-quarter reading within the country and compared with all other disadvantaged students. On the three-part wellbeing composite there is generally no difference. For life satisfaction alone (share answering 7 or more), a larger share of resilient students is satisfied in Jordan, Lebanon and the Philippines, and the reverse holds in Brazil, Kazakhstan, Kosovo and on average across OECD countries. Resilient students also report less meaning in life and more belonging. The chapter gives no magnitude in the text, uses no controls and summarises the evidence as mildly favourable to resilient students.
- **The inverted U.** PISA 2018 Volume III (chapter 11) reports that students answering 9 or 10 score lower in reading than other students once socio-economic status is accounted for. Li and Ho (2024) and Siebecke (2026), both in *Child Indicators Research*, find the same curvilinear relation; an NFER report on PISA 2018 (Kuhn 2021 in the bibliography) describes a negative pupil-level relation between attainment and life satisfaction in parts of the United Kingdom.
- **Use of the answer 10 by achievement.** Barrington-Leigh (2024) shows in PISA 2018, pooled over 72 countries, that the relative frequency of the answers 0, 5 and 10 falls with mathematics proficiency.

What was not found: a study that compares resilient students with disadvantaged low achievers, within schools, with socio-emotional skills held fixed, across many systems, or that locates the gap on the ladder with a threshold model. Siebecke (2026), the closest article in the target journal, models wellbeing as a predictor of achievement within the resilient and non-resilient groups in Sweden and does not estimate the level gap.

**Consequence.** The contribution cannot be "resilient students are less satisfied". It is the size and uniformity of the gap against the relevant comparison group, the masking by skills, and the demonstration that the gap is a shortfall of top answers whose interpretation is bounded. Clarke (2020) objects in print to reading achievement–wellbeing differences as a trade-off; the title's word "cost" has to answer that.

### H1. The cost

The anchors named in the report exist but do not say what the hypothesis needs.

- **Skin-deep resilience.** Brody et al. (2013) and Miller et al. (2015, *PNAS*) are confirmed. In both, disadvantaged young people with high competence or self-control had *better* psychological outcomes (fewer depressive symptoms, fewer internalising problems); the cost was physiological (allostatic load, epigenetic ageing). Later studies repeat this (Brody et al. 2016, 2020; Chen et al. 2015, 2020; Miller et al. 2020; Gaydosh et al. 2018), and the field's own review says upward mobility typically brings better mental health alongside worse physical health (Chen et al. 2022). None measures life satisfaction. The physical cost appears mostly in Black and Latinx samples in the United States and is absent for white participants and in Britain (Miller et al. 2016; Präg & Richards 2019).
- **Average association.** Two meta-analyses find a small positive correlation between achievement and subjective wellbeing (Bücker et al. 2018, r = 0.16; Kaya & Erdem 2021). PISA 2015 reported top achievers marginally more satisfied than low achievers on OECD average (OECD 2017). A systematic review in *Child Indicators Research* calls the evidence inconsistent (Amholt et al. 2020).
- **Evidence in the hypothesised direction.** Lower life satisfaction or more school stress with higher attainment in England and Sweden (Clarke & McLellan 2024; Klapp et al. 2024); emotional distress among adolescents who look resilient on behavioural competence (Luthar 1991; Luthar et al. 1993); bright low-income children in the United Kingdom about 0.3 SD less happy at 14 than bright high-income peers (Jerrim & Palma Carvajal 2025). The last compares with advantaged high achievers, not with disadvantaged low achievers.

**Assessment.** As worded, H1 is contradicted or qualified by most of the charted literature and supported directly only by the OECD (2019) average. The paper's result is new relative to the skin-deep-resilience literature, which predicts good self-reported wellbeing among disadvantaged achievers.

### H2. Masking

Both premises have support, with one qualification.

- **Skills and relationships raise life satisfaction.** OECD (2021, 2024) and Guo et al. (2023) find every skill positively related to life satisfaction, optimism most strongly, followed by trust, energy, stress resistance and emotional control. Belonging, family support and freedom from bullying are consistent correlates in PISA, HBSC and Children's Worlds (OECD 2019 Volume III; Bi et al. 2021; Savahl et al. 2019; Lee & Yoo 2015).
- **Resilient students have more of some of them.** OECD (2019) reports more self-efficacy, mastery motivation, enjoyment of reading and belonging among resilient students. Belonging does not predict resilience everywhere (Siebecke 2024 for Sweden; Ye et al. 2021).
- **Qualification.** The skills that go with achievement (curiosity, persistence, self-control) are not the ones that go most with life satisfaction (optimism, trust, emotional regulation) (Guo et al. 2023; OECD 2021). PISA 2025 measures the first kind. The masking the paper estimates is therefore masking by task-oriented skills; the skills most tied to life satisfaction are unobserved.

No study was found that conditions an achievement–wellbeing gap on socio-emotional skills or names suppression. The masking step appears to be new.

### H3. Not general response style

The literature names three rival mechanisms; the report addresses two of them.

- **Response style and achievement within countries.** The only multi-country PISA analysis found (Lu & Bolt 2015, PISA 2006) shows extreme responding essentially unrelated to achievement within countries, which supports H3. A single-country PISA 2022 study finds extreme style associated with lower mathematics scores (İlgün Dibek & Yildirim-Erbasli 2026). Ulitzsch et al. (2023) show that conclusions depend on how style is measured, so one index is thin evidence.
- **Careless responding.** Inconsistent or random responding is more common among low achievers and students from poorer homes in TIMSS (Steinmann et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2023), and questionnaire effort correlates with test scores in PISA (Zamarro et al. 2019). Studies that remove careless responders report small changes in results (Steedle et al. 2019; Calderon & Gehlbach 2021). The report's speeder exclusion addresses this channel.
- **Focal answers.** Barrington-Leigh (2024) argues that less numerate respondents simplify the 0–10 scale to its bottom, middle and top, and shows the pattern in PISA 2018 by mathematics score. This contradicts H3 as worded and is not covered by the report's checks. The effort-scale placebo speaks against a general habit of choosing 10, but effort is not content-free and the placebo does not test 0 and 5.

**The focal-answer reading is testable in the paper's own data.** It predicts that disadvantaged low achievers over-use 0 and 5 as well as 10. Unweighted first-plausible-value shares seen during the threshold work point that way (0: 2.6% against 1.5%; 5: 10.0% against 7.0%; 10: 30.0% against 15.6%), and so do the threshold shifts at "5 or more" and "6 or more" in step 8. No estimate has been made.

### H4. Specificity

Most of the charted evidence runs against a larger psychological cost for disadvantaged students.

- **Against.** The skin-deep-resilience studies find psychological outcomes better, not worse, for low-status strivers. Quantitative mobility studies mostly reject a wellbeing penalty of upward mobility (Chan 2018; Dhoore et al. 2019; Houle & Martin 2011; Zang & de Graaf 2016; Patzina et al. 2026). The "high-achieving schools" literature places pressure costs among affluent youth (Luthar et al. 2013, 2020). Quasi-experimental evidence on grading in Sweden finds harm mainly among low-to-moderate achievers (Linder et al. 2025).
- **For.** Among German Gymnasium students, achievement was related to life satisfaction only where the mother had at least the education the student was pursuing (Crede et al. 2015): no return for the upwardly mobile, though not a penalty. Hadjar and Samuel (2015) find a life-satisfaction penalty of upward mobility in the United Kingdom and none in Switzerland. Friedman (2016, confirmed), Ingram (2011) and Reay et al. (2009) give the qualitative mechanism.
- **Method.** Breen and Ermisch (2024) argue that mobility effects are not identified as such and should be framed as a destination effect that varies by origin, which is exactly the form of H4. Schuck and Steiber (2018) show that estimated mobility effects on wellbeing depend heavily on specification.
- **On the instrumental-variable design.** Relative age raises adolescent life satisfaction directly (Fumarco et al. 2020, HBSC: 0.168 SD per twelve months), which bears on the exclusion restriction of a school-entry-cutoff instrument. Dee and Sievertsen (2018) find the benefits of later school entry concentrated among advantaged children, on a parent-rated outcome. This concerns the companion paper's design and has been passed on to it.

**Assessment.** The paper's own result (a difference in the cross-section that the causal design does not confirm) fits a contested literature. H4 should be stated as a contest between predictions, not as an expectation.

### RQ1. Context

The literature gives real priors for moderation, so the paper's null is informative.

- **For moderation.** The one test of a big-fish-little-pond effect on life satisfaction found (Rathmann et al. 2018, German NEPS) shows high performers less satisfied in higher-performing classes. Low-income students show more psychosocial problems among better-off schoolmates (Crosnoe 2009); relative deprivation within schools is tied to symptoms (Elgar et al. 2013); marginal entrants to selective schools feel marginalised (Pop-Eleches & Urquiola 2013); class composition affects achievement emotions (Pekrun et al. 2019).
- **Against.** A scoping review finds low socio-economic position more harmful in poorer schools, not richer ones (Herke et al. 2022). Regression-discontinuity studies find no mental-health cost, or a benefit, of entering a higher-achieving school (Bütikofer et al. 2023; Kuuppelomäki 2021). Only 5–9% of the variance in wellbeing lies between schools in PISA 2015 (Govorova et al. 2020). High aspirations carry no long-term emotional cost once attainment is held fixed (Reynolds & Baird 2010) and go with higher life satisfaction among poor adolescents in Peru (Graham & Pozuelo 2023).
- **Gender.** Girls report lower life satisfaction than boys in nearly all PISA systems (OECD 2017; Marquez 2024; Campbell et al. 2021), and high expectations raise negative affect through fear of failure more for girls (Cashman et al. 2024). No study of gender moderation of the achievement–life-satisfaction link was found.
- **Anchor check.** Marsh and Hau (2003) is confirmed; its outcome is academic self-concept, not wellbeing, and its abstract does not name the data source.

No PISA study was found that tests school-average achievement, or a status-by-achievement interaction, on life satisfaction.

### RQ2. Skills as buffers

- **Self-control is not the psychologically costly trait.** In the skin-deep-resilience studies the trait is teacher-rated competence or self-control, striving or high-effort coping, and it goes with better psychological outcomes; the cost is physiological. Direct tests find self-control positively related to wellbeing with no inverted U (Wiese et al. 2018; Hofmann et al. 2014), and high-effort coping is, if anything, protective for adolescent depression (Kahsay & Mezuk 2022). A self-regulation skill that shrinks the gap is what this literature would predict. The report's sentence that the pattern "runs against the skin-deep-resilience account" should be removed.
- **Curiosity.** No adolescent study was found in which curiosity or openness lowers life satisfaction. Openness shows no robust link in either direction (Xu et al. 2017; Winzer et al. 2021; Mõttus et al. 2024; Jovanović & Gavrilov-Jerković 2014). The nearest result is OECD (2021): with other skills held fixed, 15-year-olds who rate themselves more creative report lower life satisfaction.
- **Who rates the skill.** Self-rated skills explain several times more variance in psychological outcomes than parent- or teacher-rated skills (Guo et al. 2023). Self-rated self-regulation depends on the rater's reference group and standards (Lira et al. 2022). Both support the report's caution about moderators that hold only within self-report.

No study was found that tests a skill or personality moderator of the achievement–wellbeing relation in adolescents.

### RQ3. A different standard for the top of the ladder

The literature turns the two readings in the report into three.

- **A stricter standard among the more able or educated.** In adults, reference points for life satisfaction drift upward with education (Kristoffersen 2018), aspirations offset about half of the happiness return to education (Clark et al. 2015), and vignette studies find the more educated rating the same state more negatively (Bago d'Uva et al. 2008; Molina 2016; Angelini et al. 2014). For children, Vonkova (2019, *Child Indicators Research*, Czech fifth-graders) finds that the life-satisfaction advantage of pupils with excellent grades grows after anchoring-vignette correction. This is the reading RQ3 proposes, and the vignette method has already been applied to children's life satisfaction in the target journal.
- **A coarser use of the scale among the less able.** Barrington-Leigh (2024), as under H3. Dolan et al. (2011) note that adults answering 10 are older, poorer and less educated than those answering 9.
- **A real difference near the top.** Oishi et al. (2007) find the most successful in education and income slightly below the top of the happiness scale and read it as motivation; Ferrante (2009) reads the education penalty as unmet aspirations. Very high life satisfaction is adaptive in adolescence (Suldo & Huebner 2006), so a shortfall at the top is not harmless.
- **Identification.** Rankings of group means on ordinal scales are not identified without assumptions (Bond & Lang 2019; Schröder & Yitzhaki 2017), and the vignette assumptions fail in some tests (Bago d'Uva et al. 2011). Three usable tools were found: medians and other quantiles are identified where means are not (Chen et al. 2022); bounds robust to monotone rescaling (Bloem 2022); and response times as information on the latent variable among people giving the same answer (Liu & Netzer 2023). The student extract already holds the time spent on the life-satisfaction screen.
- **The item.** The satisfaction question used in PISA attracts more answers at the ends than the ladder format does (Marquez et al. 2026).

No vignette study of life satisfaction in PISA, HBSC or Children's Worlds was found, and no direct test in adolescents of a higher standard among high achievers apart from Vonkova (2019).

## 5. Proposed rewording of the hypotheses

Proposals only; the report is unchanged.

**H1.** *Among disadvantaged students, high achievers are less likely than low achievers in the same school to report the highest life satisfaction, and report lower life satisfaction on average.*
Framed as a test between predictions: a small positive achievement–wellbeing association (Bücker et al. 2018; Kaya & Erdem 2021) and good self-reported adjustment among disadvantaged achievers (Chen et al. 2022) on one side; the inverted U and the OECD average on the other (OECD 2019; Li & Ho 2024; Siebecke 2026). Drop skin-deep resilience as support for a subjective cost.

**H2.** *The gap is larger once socio-emotional skills and relationships are held fixed, because resilient students have more of the skills and relationships that go with life satisfaction.*
Anchors: OECD (2019, 2021, 2024); Guo et al. (2023). State that PISA 2025 measures task-oriented skills and not the emotional-regulation skills most tied to life satisfaction.

**H3.** *The gap is not produced by differences between the two groups in (a) extreme, acquiescent or midpoint responding, (b) careless responding, or (c) a coarser use of the 0–10 scale that concentrates answers on 0, 5 and 10.*
Parts (a) and (b) are tested. Part (c) is new and untested; until it is, the verdict on H3 is "supported for (a) and (b), open for (c)". Anchors: Lu & Bolt (2015); Steinmann et al. (2024); Barrington-Leigh (2024).

**H4.** *The life-satisfaction penalty attached to high achievement differs by family background.* Two-sided.
Predictions: larger among disadvantaged students (Crede et al. 2015; Friedman 2016; Hadjar & Samuel 2015); no difference or larger among advantaged students (Chan 2018; Dhoore et al. 2019; Luthar et al. 2020). Framing: Breen & Ermisch (2024).

**RQ1.** Unchanged in wording. Anchors replaced: Rathmann et al. (2018) and Crosnoe (2009) for moderation; Herke et al. (2022), Bütikofer et al. (2023) and Govorova et al. (2020) against. Marsh and Hau (2003) only as the origin of the idea, for self-concept.

**RQ2.** Unchanged in wording. Remove the claim that the self-regulation result contradicts skin-deep resilience. Add Guo et al. (2023) and Lira et al. (2022) as the reason to distrust self-report moderators.

**RQ3.** *Why do resilient students give fewer top answers: lower satisfaction, a stricter standard for a 9 or a 10, or a finer use of the scale than low achievers?*
Three readings, with Vonkova (2019), Kristoffersen (2018) and Barrington-Leigh (2024) as their sources and Bond and Lang (2019) for the identification problem.

## 6. Analyses the review suggests

Item 1 was run on 14 November 2026 as step 9 (`01_code/09_focal.R`; results in `01_code/README.md`): low achievers do over-use 0 and 5, and coarse scale use accounts for roughly a fifth to two fifths of the shortfall of 10s. The other items have not been run. Each uses data already in the extract.

1. **Focal-answer test (H3c, RQ3).** The gap in the share answering 0, 5 or 10 jointly and separately; the gap among students who give a non-focal answer; whether focal answering falls with mathematics score within the disadvantaged quartile. A mixture model in the manner of Barrington-Leigh (2024) is the fuller version.
2. **Quantile comparison (RQ3).** Median and upper-quantile differences between the two groups, which are identified where the mean difference is not (Chen et al. 2022).
3. **Response time on the life-satisfaction screen (RQ3).** Whether resilient students who answer 8 or 9 differ in response time from low achievers who answer 10 (Liu & Netzer 2023). The variable is in the student extract but not in the analytic file, so this needs a change in the shared preparation, which this paper's session may not make.
4. **A second style index (H3a).** Ulitzsch et al. (2023) show results depend on how style is measured; the report uses one family of indices.

## 7. Gaps in the literature that the paper can claim

- No estimate of the resilient-versus-disadvantaged-low-achiever gap in life satisfaction within schools, in any dataset.
- No study conditioning an achievement–wellbeing gap on socio-emotional skills.
- No multi-country within-country analysis of response style, careless responding or top-category use by achievement in PISA, apart from Lu and Bolt (2015) for extreme style and one appendix figure in Barrington-Leigh (2024).
- No test of school-average achievement or of a status-by-achievement interaction on life satisfaction in PISA.
- No skin-deep-resilience study with life satisfaction as the outcome, and none outside the United States.
